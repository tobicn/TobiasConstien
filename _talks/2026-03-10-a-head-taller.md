# Abstract

Executive functions emerge gradually in early childhood and are predictive of a range of developmental outcomes, including children’s school readiness and academic success.  Consequently, executive functions, including its sub-components of inhibitory control, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, have been identified as key targets of early intervention programs. Within this context, pretend play, a type of play that is imaginative and creative, has been posited to benefit children’s early development of executive functions in various previous theoretical conceptualisations and intervention programs. However, previous studies on the relation between pretend play and executive functions have not consistently evidenced a clear relation between the two. This study aimed to quantitatively assess the relation between pretend play and executive functions in early childhood across previous studies via a systematic review and meta-analysis for the first time. A previously piloted and pre-registered ([osf.io/v28fp](osf.io/v28fp)), systematic literature search was conducted across five databases (i.e., ERIC, ProQuest Dissertation and Theses Index, PsycInfo, Scopus, Web of Science). The screening process identified 845 results, out of which 26 studies met the pre-specified inclusion criteria. Overall, the included studies reported on 2,915 children, aged 12-72 months and provided 131 effect sizes for the analysis. A three-level, random effects meta-analysis across all included effect sizes identified a significant, small effect between executive function and pretend play, *r* = .17, 95% CI [.13, .20], *SE* = 0.02, *t*(130) = 9.93, *p* < .001, indicating that as children’s pretend play becomes more complex, their executive functioning skills become more mature, and vice versa. The identified relation was robust to outliers and showed no evidence of publication bias across several methods (i.e., funnel plot, trim-and-fill, p-curve). Moderation analyses showed that effect sizes were significantly larger for studies measuring executive functions via a questionnaire (*r* = .26, 95% CI [.17, .35]) compared to performance-based tasks (*r* = .15, 95% CI [.11, .18]). Other considered variables (i.e. social aspects of pretend play measures, age, socioeconomic status, study design), however, did not, on their own, affect the observed relation between pretend play and executive functioning in early childhood. While these results do not evidence causality, they invite further research, specifically focused on younger children (i.e., toddlers) and longitudinal designs, to build on the extant literature and fill identified gaps within the current evidence base. Based on the findings from this review, the proposed talk will present a bidirectional model on the relation between executive functions and pretend play, in which  pretend play provides a context in which to apply and practice executive function skills, and executive functions facilitate children engaging in pretend play (i.e., representational competence). The implications, both for practice and research, of this proposed model will be discussed in terms of the direction and the potential mechanisms for the identified relation between pretend play and executive functions
