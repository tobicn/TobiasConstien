I recently had the opportunity to contribute a brief presentation on observational approaches to executive function measurement at a virtual meeting of the *Early Executive Functions Global Network*. Rather than giving a summary of previous research, the talk was meant to stimulate a discussion for the group. Accordingly, the following is not meant as a comprehensive introduction to the topic, but rather a loose collection of key points that interest me about observational approaches to executive functions measurement.

## Executive functions are not static

One thing I have noticed about doing a PhD is that I find myself writing the same things over, and over again (that is what you get for dedicating three years to one single topic). One of these things is this: *"Executive functions are predictive of a range of social, health, and behavioural outcomes"* (Constien et al., 2026, p. 1). It is a nice way to illustrate the importance of early executive functions research, and backed up by a wealth of evidence (see Stucke & Doebel ([2024](#references)) for a review). Consequently, this sentence appears in some shape or form not only in my own writing, but also in basically any other paper on children's early executive functions development.

And while this statement is arguably true, it somewhat neglects to mention that  toddlers' executive functioning not only predicts behaviour at some later time, but already in toddlerhood. Just take these illustrations as an example:

- A toddler that is able to control their impulses better, may also be less likely to run across a red light (and not require a parent's constant supervision).

- A toddler with better cognitive flexiblity, may also find it easier to move on from play time to bed time (and complain less about having to brush their teeth).

- A toddler with better working memory, may be better equipped to follow their parents' instructions (which may improve parent-child interactions).

**In short**: Executive functions matter for toddlers' everyday, moment-to-moment behaviours.

## When it rains, it pours

Parent-report questionaires are already utilising this evident link between behaviours and underlying executive function skills. The first item on the Early Executive Functioning Questionnaire (Hendry & Holmboe, [2021](#references)) for instance ask parents to report the prevalence of when their child stopped *"reaching completely for something when you said no/don't touch (or similar)"*. Within the context of the EEFQ, this is seen as an indicator of toddlers' inhibitory control.

Yet, in reality, toddlers' behaviours are rarely based on a single, underlying skill of executive function or other regulatory skills. Even within the simple example of a toddler responding to their caregiver's command - *"no/don't touch* - there are a number of skills required that may be connected to inhibitory control, but could also task other abilities, like emotion regulation, or pro-social behaviour, that are further removed from inhibitory control.

Evidently, toddlers don't really care about the purity of our executive function measures (rude!). In their everyday life and behaviour, it is rare for them to be in situations that only require a single executive function skill. For example, they may be asked to stop playing, get dressed, and go outside, requiring them to remember instructions, ignore distractions, stay on task, move from one activity to the next. Even within controlled laboratory settings, and standardised task instructions, task impurity is often identified as a limiting factor (Bunge, [2024](#references)). 

In sum, **executive functioning is not static**. It is based on multiple skills, like working memory, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control, working in tandem, moment to moment. This impurity challenges measurement approaches, which is often based on the premise that we can map certain behaviours onto distinc, underlying executive function skills (e.g., *"no/don't touch"* = inhibitory control).

## Scaffolding

Children's moment-to-moment behaviour, however, does not only depend on what toddlers do, but importantly with *whom*. This also extends to children's executive functioning, and, by extension, their overall display of self-regulation. Specifically, Vygotsky's **Zone of Proximal Development** posits that there is a delta in between children's own self-regulatory skills, and the level of their exhibited self-regulation when engaged in interactions with a competent other, who can guide and scaffold children's behaviour (Vytosky, [1978](#references)).

This delta between what can be achieved alone vs. with a competent other may be particularly important in early development, in which infants and toddlers are somewhat dependent on their parents and caregivers in regard to their own regulation of behaviour and emotions. For example, it is parent's care and attention that can soothe a toddler after getting upset, or, conversely, it is parent's lack of attention that signals a toddler that this event, like falling down, is not worth crying over. Sticking with the example from the EEFQ, it may be also be the specific tone within parent's command of *do/don't touch* that signals urgency and prompt a toddler to halt their movement.

The Zone of Proximal Development illustrates that exhibited self-regulation is ultimately context-dependent, meaning it can fluctuate across enviornments and social interactions. Observational approaches to the measurement of executive functions are based on this premise, which I summarised under the metaphor of *"when it rains, it pours"*.

## Observational approaches

The core idea of observational measures is evident: If children's everyday behaviours are reliant on their executive function skills, let's use observations of their behaviours to derive a judgment of their executive function skills. In this regard, observational approaches are similar to parent-reported measures, like the EEFQ. However, there are also important differences. First, the window of time that can (realistically) be observed is shorter. Secondly, the judgment is made by someone further removed from the child (i.e., a researcher, not a parent). Consequently, the benefits of observational approaches to parent-reported measures are that (1) observational ratings made by a researcher are arguably more objective, and (2) researchers have the advantage of a broad comparison group against which to judge a child's behaviour relative to a parent, who in most cases only experiences their own child.

A major drawback to observational approaches, however, is that they, by design, can only capture what is being observed. This means they are tied to observed, outwardly displayed behaviours and cannot operationalise the specific underlying executive skill or motivation behind the behaviour. Accordingly, observational tools cannot make a judgement on a child's level of inhibitory control, or working memory, for instance, as they can only refer to observed behaviours, which depend on multiple underlying skills (e.g., inhibitory control *and* working memory), the context, as well as the provided social support. The same challenges around task impurity, consequently, also apply to observational measures.

Still, there are two major advantages to observational approaches, which make them particularly interesting to me. First, observational measures are generally marked by a high level of **ecological validity**, which refers to a measure's ability to capture processes that are relevant to everyday behaviours. Arguably in this regard, observational approaches are beating out relatively abstract tasks of executive functioning and are on somewhat equal footing with parent-reports. After all, they are based on everyday behaviours.

Secondly, scores from an observational approaches are intuitelvey interpretable. **Interpretability** refers to the ability to link derived scores with concrete behaviours. Interpretable scores are important because their relevance is easily understood and communicated to parents, educators, or policy-makers. Observational approaches are at an advantage here. Observational scores that, for example, clearly show how often a toddler listend to instructions from their parent, stopped at a red light, or disengaged from a tablet within a short period of time, are easy to make sense. Conversely, task-based scores often lack this direct link to concrete behaviours. For instance, it is not clear what benefit a toddler, who sorted five out of six bears correctly in the reverse categorisation task, has in their everyday life compared to a toddler who sorted only two bears correctly.

Given these advantages, there's recently been an increasing interest in utilising observational approaches within the context of executive functioning and self-regulation - though older examples also exist. In my area of research, namely pretend play and executive functioning, there is an often cited study from 2002 that operationalised pre-schoolers self-regulation as *"the extent to which the child takes responsibility for picking up his or her toys"* (Elias & Berk, [2002](#references), p. 224). More recently, there's been the **Regulation-Related Skills Measure** from Dana McCoy ([2019](#references)). It uses a set of eleven items such as *"pays attention"*, or *"ignores distractions"* on which children are rated on a four-point Likert scale to derive a score of pre-schoolers' attention control and inhibitory control. A similar approach is used by the **Self-Regulation in Play Scale** (Slot et al., [2017](#references)), which identifies cognitive and emotional self-regulation in pre-schoolers play using indicators like persistence, or knowledge of emotions.

## What's next?

To my knowledge, there has not been an observational measure of toddlers' executive functioning and self-regulation. Although the Self-Regulation in Play Scale has been used with children as young as two, it was developed to be used for preschoolers and within preschool settings. In my research, I am exploring toddlers' pretend play and executive functioning, which includes an observation of dyadic parent-child free play. Building on the items developed for the Regulation-Related Skill Measure, I am intending to develop and test an observational regulation measure for toddlers.
