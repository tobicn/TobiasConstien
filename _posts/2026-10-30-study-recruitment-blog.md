At the **UCD Babylab**, we are conducting research looking at various elements of children’s early development, like their play, spatial skills, or screen use. Of course, none of this work would be possible without you, the parents and caregivers who generously give their time to fill out a survey, or visit us in person with their child at the lab. You might have seen multiple social media posts from us, recruitment flyers at your local library, or received emails asking you and your child to participate in our studies. We promise, we don’t mean to be annoying, we just want to do good science. *Let me tell you why:*

## Sample size, statistical power, and research quality

There are multiple factors on which a research study is being judged, one of them being its sample size (Bornstein et al., [2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dr.2013.08.003)). The sample, that is you and your child, as well as all the other families participating in our research. Generally, the bigger the sample of a study, the more valid are its findings, meaning it can accurately reflect the real world. This is particularly relevant for developmental studies. As you very much know, a multitude of things are influencing children’s everyday behaviour and their development. Moreover, these influences often differ between families or even between siblings. As we are not focusing on one specific child, we need to control for all of these various influences in order to get real results that accurately represent the wider population. Through statistical analyses, we can control for this variability, however, only when we have a large enough sample!

Being able to control for noise means that a statistical analysis has sufficient *power*. You can think of statistical power like a jar of marbles. Most marbles are white. That’s the statistical noise of children’s everyday environments we are trying to filter out. Some marbles within the jar, however, are red. Those marbles are the true effects we are trying to detect in our studies, like for example the association between children’s play and their cognitive skills. The bigger the sample, the more often we get to take a marble out of the jar and the more likely it is that, when we count it all up, we can find some red marbles. Conversely, with only a small scoop of marbles, we might, erroneously, conclude that that there are no red marbles and thus miss the real effects. In sum, the bigger the sample within our studies, the better our analysis is powered and the more valid are our results.

Additionally, it is also important to ensure that a sample is not only large but also diverse. Across psychology, researchers often rely on so-called “convenience” samples, which are, as the name suggests, convenient, meaning they are people or families that are close by and thus easy to recruit (Doebel & Frank, [2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/15248372.2023.2270055)). This often results in research participants being university students or staff. While this might result in a large sample, it also risks that research findings are not readily generalisable to populations beyond the university context. Therefore, we aim to connect with families beyond the UCD campus. It is how we ensure that our findings actually reflect the real world your child is growing up in.

Of course, there are many ways to get a big, diverse sample – and not all of them include repeatedly posting on Instagram. A prevalent approach within psychology is to use crowdsourcing platforms like Prolific or MTurk. These are sites that allow researchers to *hire* people to fill out online surveys or participate in an online research task. While this is an easy way to get a large sample in a relatively short time, it has also been found to have severe shortcomings. Often times, participants from these platforms are not fully paying attention to the studies they are participating in, or they are gaming the system and falsely report characteristics in order to take part in more studies and get paid. For example, they may claim to be a parent of a toddler and fill out questions on their development and behaviour, even though they don’t really have one. More recently, there have also been increasingly concerns about bots and AI being used to complete studies on crowdsourcing platforms which can severely limit a studies validity.

Consequently, at the Babylab, we avoid these platforms and instead use more traditional recruitment approaches for our studies in order to ensure we get “real” participants. We often partner with hospitals, local libraries, crèches, toddler groups or schools to directly engage with families like yours. We also go out to community events like the UCD Festival or the Dublin Maker to inform about our work.

In sum, when we are posting about our research studies, inviting you to participate, or emailing you directly, it is because we want to conduct good research that accurately reflects children’s development in Ireland, and, ultimately, can inform policy and practice to ensure a positive development for all children. *Would you like to help us?*

That’s all.
